Poll: Sen. Rounds leads challenger Bengs 56-37%
October 22, 2026
Independent Brian Bengs and incumbent Republican U.S. Sen. Mike Rounds. 

Race includes second-time Senate hopeful Independent Brian Bengs challenging incumbent Republican U.S. Sen. Mike Rounds, who's seeking his third term.

Republican Sen. Mike Rounds leads Independent challenger Brian Bengs 56% to 37%, according to the latest South Dakota Poll sponsored by News Watch and the Chiesman Center for Democracy at the University of South Dakota.

The Oct. 12-14 survey of 500 likely voters by Mason-Dixon Polling and Strategy, ranked as one of the least-biased and most-factual pollsters, also found that 62% of independents support Bengs, while 30% support Rounds and 8% of independents are undecided.

More states have independents running for federal offices across the country this year.

Other findings from the U.S. Senate poll:

Thirty-four percent of respondents did not recognize Bengs' name.
Bengs (46%) and Rounds (47%) effectively split younger voters, aged 18-34.
Thirty percent of independents polled said they would choose Rounds, a Republican, over Bengs, an Independent.
About two-third of respondents approve of Rounds' job performance as senator; one-third don't approve.

Rob Skjonsberg, campaign adviser for Rounds, said South Dakotans know and trust Rounds, who served in the Legislature, was governor from 2003 to 2011 and has been one of the state's two U.S. senators since 2015.

"On Nov. 3, the footnote of this race is going to be about the fact that this was about common sense versus crazy," Skjonsberg said. "That's going to be the footnote."

Analysis

Here are some observations from News Watch partners, political scientists and the candidates' campaigns.

Bengs' second Senate bid has unclear impact

Bengs saw slightly higher name recognition than two other candidates running against Republicans for office, Democrats Dan Ahlers and Nikki Gronli.

Emily Wanless, associate professor of government and international affairs at Augustana University in Sioux Falls, said some of that recognition could be through Bengs' previous bid for Senate, where he ran against Republican U.S. Sen. John Thune as a Democrat in 2022.

Bengs has also seen more national media attention as he campaigns with other independent Senate hopefuls Dan Osborn of Nebraska and Seth Bodnar of Montana.

"With Bengs, I do think the independent caucus and the national attention that he's gotten, the general interest in voters exploring outside of the two-party candidates, might garner him some attention as well," Wanless said. "I don't know that come Election Day everyone who identifies as an independent is really acting independently. But they like the idea of being open to a candidate outside of the two parties, and so he might be getting some traction from that.

"Bengs, while technically a loser in in his previous Senate bid, developed a campaign infrastructure and developed name recognition across the state, such that like a ground league just doesn't have yet. So it's one thing to be outside of politics; it's another thing to be outside of politics but having political aspirations in the past that will increase name recognition," Wanless said.

The South Dakota Poll results have Bengs in a different spot than some others released by the Bengs campaign and other groups. A poll by Concord Public Opinion Partners showed Bengs and Rounds effectively tied at 43% to 42%.

"The Bengs campaign (has) spent over $100,000 on polling from various pollsters to get an accurate read of where the race is. Some have us up, some have us down. But the fact is that Mike Rounds' allies wouldn't be dumping so much money into this race if they really thought it was safe," Clara Bronikowski, a representative from the Bengs campaign, said to News Watch in a statement.

Rounds maintains high approval rating

Julia Hellwege, associate professor of political science at the University of South Dakota in Vermillion, said that Rounds has a relatively high approval rating for a sitting senator, especially when compared to the approval rating of other national politicians like Thune and President Donald Trump – results of those poll questions are forthcoming.

Hellwege said that Rounds' activity in Congress – which has stayed under the radar compared to Thune, who has served as Senate majority leader since January 2025 – could be helping him maintain favor among South Dakotans.

"Rounds is the junior senator. He also was a beloved governor who did really well as governor. That protects him, but he also really stays away from some of the more D.C.-esque concerning things. I think Rounds has very purposefully, maybe even strategically, tried to to stay away from falling into that trap, if you will. And so I'm not surprised by by that at all," Hellwege said.

Another South Dakotan Congress member, U.S. Rep. Dusty Johnson, didn't advance from a four-person primary on June 2 and then a runoff election that Gov. Larry Rhoden won.

Johnson's performance in that race doesn't necessarily indicate how South Dakotans feel about their representatives in Washington, Hellwege said.

"At a certain point, South Dakotans feel as though you have become too 'Washington,' and they will punish politicians accordingly, even when they're in positions of power and authority that benefit South Dakota," Hellwege said.

"They feel as though they've lost kind of their home, their center. I think Rounds is immune from that because he was a state politician first and foremost. He is going to capitalize on his prior role in the state, and that is going to run counter to a trend in nationalized elections that we're seeing, where it's more about the economy and it's more about Trump. You start seeing things like Rounds coming out with ads that are referencing his time in South Dakota and his value of Ronald Reagan versus Donald Trump. And that's him trying to distance himself from what's going on in D.C."

Our polling methods

Mason-Dixon Polling and Strategy, ranked as one of the least-biased and most-factual pollsters, conducted the poll of 500 registered voters Oct. 12-14 using random selections from a telephone-matched state voter registration list that included both landline and cellphone numbers from across the state. All respondents indicated they were likely to vote in the November general election. The margin for error is no more than ± 4.5 percentage points.
